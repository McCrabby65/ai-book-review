# Epilogue: Millbrook

*Three months later*

The coach ride back from Garnerby was short, and it was the most fun I'd had in weeks. Ruta spent most of it guilt-tripping Vagid about Mara. Orrin and I mostly just laughed. Nara laughed too, but every so often she dropped a bomb.

"Mara said she's been asking Vagid when she gets to meet his parents," she said, to no one in particular.

Ruta turned slowly toward her son. Faren found something very interesting out the window.

We pulled into Millbrook in the early afternoon. Plan B and I gathered our gear while Ruta and Faren headed for the inn.

"You haven't introduced Mara to your parents yet?" said Orrin. "Why not?"

"None of your business," said Vagid.

"They'll have to get to know her eventually," I said. "I mean, she practically lives at your place now."

Vagid's ears flamed. Nara snickered. Orrin laughed so hard he had to lean on the coach.

"They'll all be there tonight at dinner," said Vagid. It wasn't the denial he thought it was.

By the time we got close to the Guild, we could see a line stretching out the doors and down the street. A farmer with his arm in a sling. A mother holding a coughing boy. An old man leaning on a cane he probably wouldn't need in an hour.

"Looks like I'll be busy for a while," I said.

"Do you need any help with the crowd?" asked Nara.

"No, the Guild has it down to a system by now. But I want to see Prue first."

"You two should just get married already," said Vagid.

"They're already like an old married couple," said Orrin.

"I think it's sweet," said Nara. "And Vagid, you may not want to mention marriage around Mara."

Vagid's ears flamed again.

Inside, Vagid got in Mara's line out of habit, even though he had nothing to turn in. I walked behind the counter to Prue's section.

She looked up from a stack of slips. "You're late."

"The coach was late." I kissed her. "How do we want to handle this crowd?"

"The same as always. I'll send them to the town center. You stand in the middle and do your thing."

"My thing."

"Your thing." She was already filling out a slip. Every slip said the same two words: *No charge.*

Prue moved the crowd the way she'd once counted forty-seven goblin ears—quickly, efficiently, and without any visible joy. In no time, the town center was packed. I walked to the middle, next to the well, and took a breath.

*Eddy, I could use a hand.*

*Always, Evan.* He didn't sound tired anymore. He hadn't for three months.

"High Heal."

The circle snapped out across the square. The sling came off. The coughing stopped. The old man looked at his cane like he couldn't remember why he'd bought it. Somebody cheered. Somebody always cheered.

Then everyone got back in line—at Prue's table, for the paperwork.

*The healing takes a second. The paperwork takes the rest of the afternoon.*

By the time the last slip was signed, the square was empty and the shadows were getting long. Prue handed the stack to Cuthbert, and we walked back toward her house. The road was still muddy from the thaw, but the trees along it had started to bud.

"We leave for the capital in a week," I said.

"I know. I wrote it on the board."

Of course she had.

"Were you able to look at any of the places I mentioned?" I asked.

"All business today, huh?" said Prue. "I did, but I don't think any of them will work. Why can't we just stay at my place?"

"I like your place, but I wanted a bit more space," I said. "You know... for little ones..."

"Ah. I see. And where would you get these little ones?" she asked.

"Well... I hoped we could... you know..." I stammered.

She was smiling wickedly now. "Could what?"

This clearly wasn't going the way I had planned, so I changed tactics.

"Have you thought about what I asked you?" I said.

"I did, but I'm not sure. Maybe I'll find a better offer," she said.

*I'm not going to win any of these.*

We walked for a bit. A cart rolled past, and the driver waved at me like he knew me. He probably did. Half the town had stood in that square at one time or another.

"Any word on when the chapel will be done?" she asked.

"I haven't been by yet. My first thought when I got back was, I need some Prue time," I said.

"Oh? And what would you do with this Prue time?"

We reached her house, went in, and closed the door.

An hour later, we were walking to the inn. The sun was almost down, and the first lamps were being lit along the street. We'd planned to meet all our friends for dinner. It was something we hoped would become a habit.

"Oh. Garrick sent you a letter," said Prue. She pulled it out of her pocket. "It came while you were in Garnerby."

The seal was plain. Garrick's handwriting was not. It was so neat it looked printed.

> *Evan,*
>
> *Your rooms in the east wing are ready. The king asks that you dine with him the first night. Wren and Mica are well. Mica has begun lessons with Wren's tutor, and the tutor has begun to regret it.*
>
> *I did not say it properly at Thornspire, so I will say it here. Thank you.*
>
> *My daughter has made you a horse. It is not a good horse. Mica and Wren helped.*
>
> *Garrick*

I laughed out loud. Prue read it over my shoulder and smiled.

"So. The capital," I said.

"Two days on a coach with you, a bunch of guards, and some party I've never heard of. How could I refuse?" she said.

"Is that a yes?" I asked.

"It's a 'someone has to keep your records straight.'" She walked a few steps. "And someone should be there when you walk into that castle."

I smiled and took her hand.

We turned the corner by the new chapel. It stood at the far end of the street, half built. The walls were up, but the roof was still bare beams, and scaffolding wrapped one whole side. Halden stood in front of it with his hands behind his back, looking up at it the way he looked at everything.

"Evening, Halden," I said.

He looked at the scaffolding. He looked at me. "Hmf."

That was all. From Halden, it was practically a blessing.

I looked up at the scaffolding too. It reminded me of a cathedral I'd seen in Europe, a lifetime ago. One spire finished and one in scaffolding. Back then, I'd thought it looked unfinished. Now it just looked like something people hadn't given up on.

"Come on," said Prue, tugging my hand. "We'll be late."

"We're never late," I said. "The coach is late."

When we got to the inn, I saw Dorran standing outside the door in full uniform, his spear straight, his eyes fixed on nothing. He'd asked for the High Cleric's guard detail the week after Thornspire, and he'd been on it ever since. In three months, I had never once seen him lean on anything.

"Dorran, how have you been?" I asked.

"Sir! Good, sir!" said Dorran.

"You're going to join us for dinner, right?" I asked.

"Sir! No, sir!" said Dorran. "I am on duty, sir!"

From inside, I could hear Orrin laughing and Ruta's voice rising over it.

"I think Ruta might have something to say about that," said Prue.

Dorran's eyes flicked toward the door. Just for a second. It was the most afraid I'd ever seen him.

Inside, the common room was warm and loud. Our friends had pushed two tables together in the back corner. Orrin was already eating. Nara sat next to him with a bundle wrapped in cloth on her lap. Ruta and Faren sat across from them, and Vagid sat between his parents looking like a man waiting to be sentenced.

Ruta spotted us and waved. Then she spotted the uniform behind us.

"Dorran."

"Mother, I am on duty."

"Sit down. You're not guarding anyone from me."

Dorran looked at me. I shrugged. Three months of guard duty, and it was the first time he'd ever looked to me for permission. He sat down.

The door opened again, and Mara swept in, cheeks pink from the cold. She went straight to our table.

"Hi, Vagid! It's so good to see you again!" Then she turned to his parents, beaming. "And it's so good to see you again too!"

She had never met them before. Ruta looked delighted anyway.

"Mother, Father," said Vagid, standing up so fast he nearly knocked over the bench. "This is Mara."

"Such a lovely girl," said Ruta. "Of course, I had to hear about her from Nara."

Nara smiled into her cup.

"Ruta," said Faren, in a very diplomatic tone. "Let the girl sit down."

Mara sat down next to Vagid, and for the next few minutes, Ruta asked her everything. Where her family was from. How long she'd worked at the Guild. What she saw in her son. That last one made the whole table go quiet.

Mara opened her mouth, but Vagid got there first.

"She's wonderful," he said.

He said it to his mother, but he was looking at Mara. His ears went red, then his neck, then the rest of his face. He didn't look away.

Mara didn't say anything at all. She just took his hand under the table. Ruta pressed a hand to her chest and, for the first time since I'd known her, had nothing to say.

Orrin broke the silence. "Did anyone else see the board? Some party called the Iron Kettles got the capital run. The Iron Kettles."

"Everyone gets a turn," said Prue. "That's what makes it fair."

"It's not fair. The Iron Kettles got lost on the way to Garnerby."

"That's why they need the practice," said Prue.

The food came, and for a while, nobody said anything important. Then Nara set her bundle on the table and pushed it toward me.

"I wanted you to see it first," she said.

I unwrapped the cloth. Inside was a small statue of white marble, no taller than my hand. It was a man of average height and average build with an ordinary, kind face. He wore plain clothes. His hands were open at his sides.

"It's a model," said Nara. "The real one will be for the chapel. I wanted to get it right first."

Orrin leaned over. "That's a god?"

"That's him," I said.

I'd described Eddy to her over and over these last three months. His face, his hair, the way he stood. I'd done my best. But I'd never told her he didn't look tired anymore. I'd never thought to.

The little marble man looked rested. Like someone who'd finally had a chance to sit down.

*She got him right anyway.*

"It's perfect, Nara," I said.

She smiled and went back to her soup.

Orrin sat back and folded his arms. "Well, that's just great. Nara makes gods. Evan heals whole towns. Vagid's got Mara." He picked up the last roll in the basket. "And I'm the worthless one who eats all the bread."

He was grinning. It was a joke. Everyone knew it was a joke.

"You'd never let me say that about myself," I said.

"That's different. You're my friend."

"Then don't say it about mine."

Orrin stopped with the roll halfway to his mouth. He looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked down at the table.

"Fair," he said quietly.

Then he tore the roll in half and gave the bigger piece to Nara.

The noise came back. Ruta started on Mara again. Faren tried to stop her. Prue's hand found mine under the table. At some point, Orrin told the story of Dorran's face at the door, and Dorran laughed so hard he had to take his helmet off. He never put it back on.

It was late when Prue and I finally left. Instead of taking the road, we walked home along the river.

The moon was up, and the water was high and fast with the spring melt. It was loud, the way rivers are in spring. A few months ago, that sound would have put my shoulders up around my ears. Now it was just the river.

Prue walked with her arm through mine, not saying anything. That was one of the things I loved about her. She never filled a silence just to fill it.

I thought about the man who'd woken up naked on a riverbank with no money, no magic, and a file that said *Cause of injury: rocks.* I thought about the room where someone had written *defective* next to my number. I thought about a chamber that wasn't there anymore.

*The past is set in stone.*

I thought about a coach to the capital in a week, and a house with more space, and a marble god with an ordinary face waiting for a chapel with a roof.

And I thought about Johan. I wondered if he'd made it to Lindmora. Maybe he was stocking shelves in that little store with the linden leaf on the sign, straightening postcards of a cathedral. I'd never know. I hoped he had stopped counting.

*The future is like a river. Don't fight the river.*

We stopped where the bank bent. Just past a big rock, the current curled back on itself into a quiet pool. The water there turned slow circles while the rest of the river rushed by.

An eddy.

"Are you talking to him again?" asked Prue.

"Just saying goodnight."

She leaned her head on my shoulder. We stood there for a while and watched the water rest.

*Every river needs a rest stop.*
