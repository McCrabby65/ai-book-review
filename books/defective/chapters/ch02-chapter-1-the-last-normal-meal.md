# Chapter 1: The Last Normal Meal

The last normal meal I ever ate was a ten-inch pizza called the Run the Meat.

The River's End was loud the way only a restaurant full of sunburned, half-dry people can be loud. Every table was replaying the same eight miles of river: who flipped where, who swam the last rapid, what they'd try tomorrow.

Our table was no quieter. Tres had ordered a burger with everything on it, a tower of a thing, and was approaching it like a man who'd done this before. Curt was losing a slow war to keep his beef sandwich inside the bun. Jocelyn cut her chicken sandwich into neat little squares. Scott eyed the sauerkraut on his Reuben with deep suspicion, then decided to make it work.

The guy across from me took a spoonful of chili and nodded. "It's good." He took another. "Not as good as mine."

I'd ordered the pizza mostly because the name made me smile. After eight miles of the river chewing on me, I figured I'd earned something with "meat" in the name.

"Okay," Tres said, tapping the table. "Best thing you learned today. Go."

"Honestly, the stuff about warm gear," I said. "If you get cold out there—"

"For Evan? Easy," Curt said through a mouthful of beef. "Feet downstream. He did a *lot* of swimming today."

Everyone laughed. I smiled the way you smile when you're pretty sure you *are* the joke, and took another slice. In fairness, I had done a lot of swimming. I'd spent most of Patton's Run looking at the river from underneath. Nobody came back to the warm gear. I hadn't really expected them to.

\* \* \*

At the next table, Walt was telling a story. He never raised his voice, on the river or off it, so when he talked, people leaned in. I did, anyway.

"Right here on the Nantahala," he was saying. "A ducky comes down through the Falls"—a ducky being an inflatable kayak, the river's version of a pool toy—"and flips. Dumps out a kid and his dad. Dad's busy chasing the boat, not watching the kid. And the kid isn't swimming. He's just drifting, feet dangling down in the water."

He paused. "Then the river just folds him over and shoves him under. One second he's there, the next he's gone."

His table had gone quiet. So had ours.

"Tennis shoes with long laces. They'd caught between the rocks on the bottom, and the whole river was pushing on him." Walt let that sit. "Lucky for him, the shoe came off. He popped up screaming, scared out of his mind. But alive."

He tapped the table. "That's why it's feet downstream, and feet *up*. Toes at the surface. You never put your feet down in moving water. The river will hold on to anything you give it."

Across from me, Curt had stopped chewing. Feet downstream. For the first time all day, it didn't sound like a joke.

Over at Walt's table, a couple of the students were slumped in their chairs. "I'm so done," one of them groaned. "My arms are jelly."

"That's because you fought the river all day," Tres called over. "Newbies always do." He pointed a fry at them. "Two summers ago I had a guy fight Patton's Run so hard he went down it backward. Still swears he meant to."

Walt answered quietly, without looking up from his plate. "You can't fight a river. You've got to go with the flow. And when you get tired, grab an eddy. They're rest stops."

I rolled my aching shoulders and decided not to mention how much fighting I'd done.

I was taking a sip of water when Jocelyn leaned across the table.

"Hey, Evan. What classes are you taking?"

"None, actually. I don't go here. A coworker did this program and wouldn't stop talking about it, so I asked if I could give it a try. I'm a software developer. Full time." *Also most of my free time. My apartment's houseplant had recently filed for divorce.*

"Oh, wow. Okay." She studied me like a museum exhibit labeled *Adult*. "So you're, like, super old or something?"

"I'm not that much older than you," I said. Which was true. It just didn't feel true.

"This is the farthest I've ever been from the city," Jocelyn said.

"I've been to almost every state," Curt said, to no one who'd asked.

Jocelyn ignored him. "What about you?"

"Not many places," I said. "Europe, once."

Jocelyn's eyes went wide. "Europe? That sounds like so much fun."

"It was." I turned my water glass a quarter turn. "Sort of. My ex and I did one of those whirlwind trips. Six countries in nine days. We only saw the highlights."

"So what do you do for fun?" Jocelyn asked. "Besides work?"

"I read a lot."

"Really? I love reading. Just not school books."

"I mostly read fantasy," I said. "Stories about people who get dropped into another world."

"Oh! My boyfriend's into that." She frowned, hunting for it. "He said it's called... issy-something?"

"Isekai." I tried not to sound like I'd been waiting all day for someone to ask.

"Yeah, that's it!"

"It's Japanese for 'different world,'" I said. "Usually the hero dies and wakes up somewhere better."

"Sounds nice," Jocelyn said. "Do they get superpowers?"

"Always. A cheat skill, a status window. Usually a castle by chapter three."

It did, honestly.

\* \* \*

By the time we got back to the campsite, the sky had gone dark blue and someone already had a fire going. Most of the group drifted toward it, students and instructors in a loose circle of camp chairs and bad jokes.

I found Walt at the edge of it. "Hey, Walt. Is it okay if I turn in?"

"Sure," he said. "Feet up."

"Okay. Night."

I was sharing a tent with Curt and Scott, which meant I had maybe an hour of quiet before Curt came in and narrated his day to us like a sports announcer. I crawled in, wormed into my sleeping bag, and fluffed my camping pillow.

Was it a pillow, though? It was about the size of a paperback. *Tomorrow,* I promised my neck, *I'm buying a real pillow.*

I lay there listening to the fire crackle and someone's laugh carry across the campsite, and for no good reason my brain decided to replay Europe. Not the good parts. Train platforms. A cathedral whose name I never learned, with one spire finished and the other still in scaffolding. A grocery store where we stopped for snacks, its displays half finished, like whoever was in charge had walked out mid-shift. A sun-curled missing-person poster by the door, some guy about my age. Katie checking the time for the next train. Always the next train.

Six countries in nine days. We'd only seen the highlights. Of Europe, or of us.

*Maybe both.* I rolled over and told my brain to shut up.

I closed my eyes and let the river noise take me.

\* \* \*

Somewhere in the dark, I realized I needed to pee. Thanks, river.

Except I couldn't hear the river.

I couldn't hear the fire either, or Curt snoring, or anything at all. And the ground felt wrong. Too flat. Too hard. My sleeping bag had apparently developed sharp edges.

I tried to sit up. Nothing happened.

I opened my eyes. Stone walls. Flickering light. A ceiling I'd never seen, and straps across my chest, my wrists, my ankles. I was lying on a table. Somewhere past my feet, something hummed like an overworked server room.

I was also naked.

"Well," I said to no one. "For a dream, it could be worse."
