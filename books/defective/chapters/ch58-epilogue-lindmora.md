# Epilogue: Lindmora

*Three months later*

Johan unlocked the back door of Lindmoras Livs before the sun was up, the way he had every morning for three months. The way he had every morning, once, a very long time ago.

The store had not changed. The same narrow aisles. The same squeak in the third cooler door. The same little linden leaf painted on the sign out front, a little more faded than he remembered. He had built this store out of wood and glue on a windowsill in Greywater Keep. He had left out the squeak. There was no way to build a squeak.

He stocked the shelves first. Flour, then coffee, then the tinned fish nobody bought but everybody expected to see. He did not need a list. His hands knew where everything went before he did.

That was why Elin had hired him. She had been nineteen when he trained her, the summer before he disappeared. For her, that was a little over a year ago. She was the manager now, and she was very good at it, and she had no idea that the gray-haired man stocking her shelves was the one who had taught her how.

"You never ask where anything goes," she had said on his first day, frowning at him over her clipboard. "Have you worked in a store like this before?"

"A long time ago," he had said. It was true.

When the shelves were done, he went to the front to open the door. The corkboard hung beside it, covered in notices: a lost cat, a bicycle for sale, a choir concert at Saint Ingrid's in Granholm. And in the corner, curling at the edges, a poster.

SAKNAD. Missing.

A young man with black hair and a mole on his left cheek smiled out at the street. Twenty-two years old. He looked like someone who had never been afraid of anything.

Johan had the same mole. It sat under gray hair now, in a face the sun and the years had worked over. Customers walked past the poster every day, and then walked past him, and nobody had ever looked twice.

*Nobody looks at old men.*

He straightened the postcards on the rack by the register, the cathedral at Granholm, one spire against a blue sky, and turned the sign on the door to Öppet.

Then he went back to work.

It was Thursday, so his mother came in a little before noon.

She always came on Thursdays. She always bought the same things: coffee, rye bread, a jar of lingonberries, and whatever was on sale that she did not need. She always wore the same blue coat. He remembered it being new.

Karin Nilsson was fifty-one years old. Johan had turned sixty sometime last winter, as near as he could figure. Sometimes, when she stood at the register counting out coins, he forgot which of them was supposed to be the parent.

"Good morning, Anders," she said.

"Good morning, Mrs. Nilsson."

"Karin. How many times do I have to tell you?"

"At least once more," he said, and she laughed.

She had laughed more in the last few weeks. The first time she had come in, three months ago, she had not laughed at all. She had stood in front of the corkboard without moving, and then she had bought coffee and gone home. He had watched her through the window until she turned the corner. Then he had gone into the back room and sat on a crate until his hands stopped shaking.

He bagged her groceries and carried them out to her bicycle, the way he did every Thursday. It was cold, but the sun was out, and the snow along the road had started to go gray.

"Spring is coming," she said.

"It is."

"Coffee went up again."

"It did. I am sorry."

"It's not your fault, Anders. You only stack it." She tucked the bread in so it would not get crushed. Then she stopped and looked at him. "Have you remembered anything else? About before?"

He had told her, the first week, that he had been away a long time and that most of it he would rather not remember. Every word of it was true. She had decided on her own that he had lost his memory. He had not corrected her.

"A little," he said. "There was a river. And some people who were kind to me when they did not have to be."

"That's something."

"It is a great deal."

She nodded, as if that settled something. "Come to dinner on Sunday. Lars will be home. You should not eat alone so much."

She had asked twice before. Both times, he had found a reason not to go.

*She is going to keep asking.*

"Thank you," he said. "I would like that."

On Sunday, he stood on his parents' front step for a long time before he knocked.

The house was yellow. It had always been yellow. The paint had started to peel under the kitchen window when he was nineteen, and his father had said every spring that he would fix it. It was still peeling.

Karin opened the door before he could knock a second time. "Anders! Come in, come in. Lars, he's here."

The hall smelled like cardamom and wet wool. Lars Nilsson stood up from his chair in the front room, shook his hand once, firmly, and sat back down. He had never been a man who said much. Johan had thought, when he was young, that it meant his father did not have much to say. He knew better now.

Karin sat him at the kitchen table. At his own place. The chair with the wobbly leg, on the side by the window.

He did not know whether to laugh or to get up and leave. So he sat very still, the way he had learned to sit in rooms where he did not belong, and he let her put food in front of him.

On the wall across from him hung a school photograph. A boy of about twelve with black hair and a mole on his left cheek, grinning with one tooth missing.

Karin saw him looking. "That's our Johan," she said. "Our son."

"From the poster," said Johan.

"Yes." She sat down across from him. "He went out for a walk one evening. A year ago last autumn. He never came home."

Lars put down his fork.

She told him about their son, then. How he had wanted to be an architect, and then a teacher, and then the manager of Lindmoras Livs, which she still did not understand. How he had built a model of the whole street out of matchboxes when he was nine. How he always took the last piece of cake and then gave half of it away.

Johan listened to all of it. Some of it he remembered. Some of it he had forgotten. Some of it he had never known, because she had never told him while he was there to hear it.

"The police say he probably drowned," said Karin. "In the lake. They say it happens." She looked down at her hands. "I just want to know if he was afraid."

*Tell her. Tell her everything.*

He could not. He knew exactly what it would do to her. A son come home as an old man, with forty years she had missed and a ledger full of names she could never be told about. That was not a gift. That was a second loss.

But he could give her something else.

"I do not think he drowned," he said slowly. "And I do not think he chose to leave. I think, wherever he went, he wanted very much to come home. And I think he knew he was loved. A boy who gives away half his cake knows he is loved."

Karin pressed her hand to her mouth.

Every word of it was true.

*This is what I can give them. Not him. Just this.*

Later, Lars walked him to the door. Johan was halfway down the step when his father spoke.

"You hold your fork the way he did."

Johan stopped. He did not turn around.

"Left-handed. Like you are holding a pen." Lars was quiet for a moment. "Karin has not laughed like that in a year. Come again next Sunday."

The door closed softly behind him.

Johan stood on the walk in the cold, with his hands in his pockets. Then he went home.

The lake would not be warm enough to swim in until June. So on Saturdays, Johan took the bus to the public baths in Granholm.

The pool smelled of chlorine and wet tile. Seven children sat along the edge in a row, kicking their feet in the shallow end, waiting for him. The youngest was five. The oldest was nine and already insisted she did not need lessons, which was why she needed them most.

He had answered a notice on the corkboard at the store, in his second week. *Volunteer wanted. Swimming lessons for children. Patience required.* He had read it four times. Then he had taken it down so nobody else would get there first.

"Arms out," he said. "Like a star. The water will hold you up if you let it."

They lay back, one at a time, and he kept a hand under each small back until they stopped fighting. Then he took the hand away, slowly, and let them float.

In the other world, the water had answered him. He had only to want it, and it rose. He had wanted it, once, on the worst day of his life, and a whole town had gone under. Here, the water did not answer him at all. It lay in the pool and did whatever water does.

He was glad.

"Anders, I'm sinking!"

"You are not sinking. You are worrying. Those are different." He put his hand back under the boy's shoulders. "Breathe. Again. There."

The other volunteers thought he was too strict. He made every child practice floating until they were bored of it, and then practice it again. He made them practice falling in with their clothes on. He made them learn how to turn onto their backs and rest when they were tired, instead of thrashing toward the edge.

"Why does it matter so much?" one of the mothers had asked him once. "They are only children."

"That is why it matters," he had said. He had not explained. He never did.

After the lesson, he took the long way home, by the lake.

The ice had started to break up along the shore. Dark water showed in long cracks between the white plates. Somewhere out there, according to the police, a young man had drowned a year ago last autumn. Johan stood at the edge and looked at the place where he was supposed to be.

Then he went home to his rented room above the bakery and took the ledger out of the drawer.

It was old, from the other world, its columns long since lost under his drawings. The first pages were Lindmora: the store, the street, the lake, and last of all the view from a windowsill in Greywater Keep. After those came the faces. Among them was a small boy, waving, with curly hair, and underneath, in Johan's careful hand: *Tam.* He knew every one.

He turned to the first empty page, picked up his pencil in his left hand, and began to draw seven children in a row at the edge of a pool, kicking their feet in the water.

On Thursday, Karin came in a little before noon.

Coffee. Rye bread. Lingonberries. A bag of licorice that was on sale and that she did not need. He rang it up, and bagged it, and carried it out to her bicycle.

The snow along the road was almost gone. Water ran in the gutters, bright with sun.

Karin tucked the bread in so it would not get crushed. Then she stopped with her hand on the handlebars.

"Lars said something to me," she said. "After you left on Sunday."

Johan waited.

"He said Anders is not your name." She said it gently, the way she used to tell him there was no more cake. "He said a man should be called by his own name at our table. So I am asking. What is it? The name you were born with."

For forty years, other people had named him. Twelve, in the castle. The Tide of Death, in the villages. Anders, which he had borrowed on his first day back, because he could not bear to hear the other one in a stranger's mouth.

In forty years, only one person had asked him that. A young man with no abilities at all, on the bank of a river.

He looked past her, through the store window, at the corkboard by the door. At the young man on the poster, who had never been afraid of anything.

Then he looked at his mother.

"Johan," he said.

Karin was quiet for a long moment. Her eyes went to the window, to the poster, and back to his face. Whatever she saw there, she did not say.

"That was my son's name," she said softly.

"It is a good name," said Johan.
